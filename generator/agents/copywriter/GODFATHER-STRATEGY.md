# PART ONE · THE STRATEGY (pp. 152–156)

## What it is (p152)

> *"This is the single most powerful, most effective, and most profit-generating strategy I
> have ever discovered… unquestionably the biggest secret I have ever uncovered in my 17 years
> in the trenches of sales and marketing."*

It instantly doubles or triples the profit of almost any sales message, in any market, and
turns the weakest traffic sources into ravenous, itchy-to-buy customers.

> # Make Your Prospects An Offer They Can't Refuse

**An offer consists of exactly two things:**

1. **What your prospects want** when they respond to your marketing
2. **What they have to do to get it**

## Where 99% get it wrong (p153)

They generate leads, or — if their head is screwed on right — offer a free report. Then they
are surprised nobody shows up waving a credit card. **They are not motivating prospects with
an irresistible offer.** Fewer than **1%** of the businesses Suby consults with have this
right before he works with them.

> **"The Godfather Strategy is about making an irresistible offer with balls. It's about
> making some huge claims and some big promises. It's about having an offer so white hot that
> it melts objections and obliterates any friction between you and the sale… and almost
> forces your prospects to buy."**

Most owners start from **weakness** — weak, watered-down promises. The impotent versions:

- *"Use our service and you might see some sort of benefit sometime in the distant future."*
- *"Download this free report, and we'll tell you how great it is to have our products."*

These "cannot withstand the competitive gale force winds that rage through online
marketplaces."

## The one unbreakable rule (p154)

Merchants once hammered out purchases face to face; the buyer haggled and asked them to
sweeten the pot. Mass marketing ended that. **Today's digital consumer is accustomed to having
your best offer presented on demand, with little to no human interaction** — and is exposed to
ever-more-amazing offers every day.

## Sell what people want to buy (p155)

Smart, educated people start from **what they themselves would like to buy**, swimming against
the river that is their market's desire. They ask *"What would I like to buy? What's
convenient for me to sell? What do I think is a cool gizmo?"*

> **They don't ask: "What is the market starving for?"**

Once the Halo Strategy has told you what people really want — **sell it to them.**

Two warnings: you can have the **right crowd at the wrong time** (the best hamburger sold to a
crowd that just ate a seven-course meal), and if what you sell is not what the market starves
for, **don't put lipstick on a pig.** Don't dress it up and fool yourself or your market.

## Good enough won't cut it (p156)

Pivot the business to align with what the market desires most. Fire employees, cut
departments, fire-sale the stock. **Do not flog a dead horse.** Short-term hardship saves
extreme long-term pain.

Most owners spend tens of thousands developing a product *before* deep research or an offer,
then mould the pitch to fit what the product can actually do — removing this claim, that
promise — **castrating the sales message from the get-go.**

> **The right way: write your sales message BEFORE ever creating the product. Make the biggest
> claims, give the market what it is starving for, create the dream-come-true experience. And
> then, and only then, develop the product to deliver on it.**

# PART TWO · BUILD THE OFFER (pp. 157–161)

## Create a Detail Sheet: Features and Benefits (p157)

Two columns. **Features** — the full list of everything you sell. **Benefits** — each feature
converted into what it does for the buyer.

> **"If you do this exercise correctly and do a good job on your research, your offers will
> almost write themselves."**

> **IMPORTANT: take a lot of time with this. Really sweat over it.** Most amateurs skip it
> entirely, and even most "top" copywriters don't labour nearly long enough over it. **List
> every single feature you can possibly think of.**

## Feature vs benefit, and the benefit chain (p158)

A **feature** is a detail or specification — the mattress is made from latex. A **benefit** is
**what it will do for the buyer** — it moulds to your body, absorbs a fidgety partner's
movement, gives undisturbed sleep.

**But push further.** Because of that latex: you sleep soundly, wake refreshed, perform better
at work, get the promotion, make more money, move to a bigger house in a nicer neighbourhood.
**All because you bought the right mattress.**

| Feature | Benefit |
|---|---|
| High-grade natural certified latex | Reduces shoulder and hip pressure · good ventilation · allergy-free · better circulation · pressure relief |
| 100% organic bamboo cotton sheets | Durable and long lasting — will save you money |
| Made from breathable material | Keeps you cool in summer and warm in winter |

Done properly this runs to **several pages**.

## Offer Basics (p159)

> *"Your offer is your basic business proposition, and is by far the most important element in
> your entire sales message."*

- What are you selling?
- How much does it cost?
- Who will take immediate action on this offer?
- How do you claim / buy it?

*Fabian's margin note: **"Pre-frame = more conversations."***

## The magic wand exercise (p160)

**This is the exercise that produces the offer.** In this order:

1. **Forget your lawyer. Forget industry regulations.** What promise would you make if you had
   a magic wand and there were **no rules or limitations** on what you could say or promise?
2. **"Even if it's not your whole service, even if it's a splinter of your core product, what
   could you give prospects that would give them some results right away?"**
3. Write the most powerful offer you can — **no more than a few lines.** Nobody sees this page
   but you, so **go over the top. Make it outrageous.**
4. **Then pull it back** to something you can actually back up, deliver on, and not be sued
   for. Offer only what you can deliver, or the promise backfires.
5. Land on something **still irresistible** but that you are confident you can deliver.

> **Irresistible offers are detailed and specific.**

## Boring vs winning, in one market (p161)

Found on Google: *"Buy your mattress online. Fast and free delivery."* Restated:
*"American-made with free delivery. Buy your mattress online today for better quality."*
Reduced: *"Buy an American-made mattress online with free and fast delivery."* Most people
would call that fine. **It is an incredibly boring offer.**

> **Casper:** *"Get America's best-reviewed mattress, delivered to your door for free, for a
> 120-night trial."*

Specificity. Risk reversal. Took Casper from zero to **over $600 million in four years** in a
stale $29 billion industry.

> Suby's aside, worth keeping: *"when we do Google searches, it's very rare that people have
> great offers, which is why it's no surprise so many businesses fail."*

# PART THREE · WEAK OFFERS, AND TWO THAT WORKED (pp. 162–166)

## The test (p162)

**Reduce the ad to a straight headline and see whether anything is left.** If it reduces to a
description of what you do, it is not an offer.

| Found | Verdict |
|---|---|
| *"Home Builders Melbourne. Designing luxury homes from a range of elegant designs. Enquire Now!"* | "Incredibly vague. It's gutless, and it will be ignored in a sea of other lame, impotent offers that provoke zero action. I don't even know what they want me to do." |
| *"Best SEO management. Digital marketing experts."* | "There's no offer here. No specificity, no risk reversal, no timeframes, no end benefit." |

## The two that worked (pp. 162–164)

> **Enso Homes:** *"We'll build your new home in just 30 weeks or give you $5,000 in cash."*

> **King Kong (his own):** *"Guaranteed Google rankings in 90 days or we work for free."*

**Neither came from thin air.** Enso's came from Halo research: the biggest pain for people
commissioning boutique luxury homes was that **builders drag out projects and miss deadlines**
— and most clients are renting, so they cannot afford to double up on rent and repayments.
*That* was the thing keeping them awake. The offer went front and centre everywhere — website,
landing pages, HVCO.

**Enso went from zero, never having taken a deposit, to over $7 million in eight months.**

King Kong's came from finding **two-thirds** of target businesses had been burned by a previous
agency and were sceptical "not necessarily of us, but of the industry as a whole."

## The offer is the tip of the spear (p165)

> **"In most cases, a strong offer will succeed in spite of weak copy, but strong copy won't
> overcome a weak offer."**

The best traffic, funnel, ads guru and heat-mapping cannot save an ordinary "good enough"
offer. **"It really is the offer that makes the heart beat and blood pump in your marketing."**

A strong offer is **not** based on price. Lowest price is trivially copied and whoever wins
that race loses. **A potent offer lets you command higher fees.**

# PART FOUR · THE SEVEN PARTS OF A GODFATHER OFFER (pp. 167–173)

### 1 · Rationale
A clear and credible explanation of **why you are making such an outrageously generous
offer.** A special introductory offer because you're confident they'll be a customer for life;
or a more efficient business model letting you pass savings on — **exposing a hoax or a con,
positioning yourself as a concerned advocate, casting stones at a shared enemy and becoming
your customer's greatest champion.**

> **"Put it up in lights and make it abundantly clear to ensure your offer is believable."**

### 2 · Build Value
Build value based on the usual everyday price — what you normally charge, or what the
competition charges.

### 3 · Pricing (p168)
Establish the regular price and make it seem like real value. **Specifics and forensic-like
detail are crucial for proof.** Say where it has sold at full price, or how many thousands
have paid it. **Show, don't tell** — screenshots of other sites and catalogues make it
believable to the everyday sceptic that you are not building superficial value or hype.

Then show why even at full price it is a **mere pittance** against what they make or save.
Then reveal the discount **in a way that illustrates your role as your prospect's advocate and
champion.**

**Reduce it down to the ridiculous** — break the price into a daily or weekly figure and
compare it with something more trivial they spend on without thinking. A cup of coffee at
$1.33 a day.

### 4 · Payment Options (p170)
When the ideal price point is high, break it into **three or four payments**. This lowers the
*perceived* price, and "today" money matters more to the decision than "tomorrow's."

**Every extra option increases the chance of losing the sale.** Any additional time spent
deciding *which* offer to take kills it.

On free consultations: **"You still need to attach a dollar amount to the consultation and you
still need to sell hard to get people to take you up on your offer, even if it's free."** It
must be specific — not "free consultation" but a **30, 45 or 60-minute** phone consultation,
analysis, strategy session or roadmap.

### 5 · Premiums (pp. 171–172)
Free gifts received *along with* the product. **A hot premium can double or triple sales.** The
advertisers spending hundreds of millions mostly run premiums, because they dramatically
increase response.

*Sports Illustrated*, 1986: promotions had bombed, so they created the **Football Phone** —
which sold **1.6 million subscriptions**.

**Great premiums are aligned and relevant to your business:**

| Business | Premium |
|---|---|
| Identity theft insurance | Free document shredder ($29 value — the LifeLock ad) |
| Homebuilding company | $5,000 furniture and appliances voucher |
| Car detailing | New floor mats |
| Moving company | "Get your bond back" repair kit, or free end-of-lease clean |
| Divorce lawyer | $500 travel voucher |

### 6 · Power Guarantee (p173)
The stronger the better. Reverses risk from prospect onto the business. Twelve-month
guarantees are most common — **but it is much better to be very specific and attach the
guarantee to the performance of what you're selling:**

> *"If you follow the program and don't lose 10kg in your first 180 days, just let us know and
> we'll refund every cent you've paid."*

### 7 · Scarcity (p173)
Offers without scarcity don't sell as well, **but it needs to be genuine or you'll erode brand
trust.** *"If you don't need to take action now, when will you take it? Never."*

- Expiration date · countdown clocks · only X left at this price · buy before X to avoid a
  price hike
- **"We only have so many hours in a day / employees to service you / products left in the
  warehouse."**

# PART FIVE · OBJECTIONS AND OFFER COPY (pp. 174–175)

## Anticipate and overcome objections (p174)

Ask what objections a **sceptical** prospect would have. Write down the main three and how
each is overcome. Then read the offer back and ask: **"What can I add to make this offer even
more compelling?"**

## Offer copy (p175)

Tightly written, direct, to the point. No confusion about **what** they receive in exchange for
money or time, **when** they receive it, and **why** it benefits them. **Edit offer copy more
ruthlessly than normal.**

# PART SIX · THE POWER GUARANTEE (pp. 176–186)

## You already have one (p178)

By law in most countries, if something is defective or doesn't perform as advertised, the
consumer is **already** entitled to repair, replacement, resupply or compensation. **So you
already have a guarantee.** If you have it anyway, why not advertise it?

> ⚠️ **Jurisdiction.** Suby cites the **ACCC** — Australian consumer law. VIP is California:
> state consumer protection plus **CSLB** contractor obligations, which are not the same.
> Any sentence about what the law already guarantees must be checked first.

## The numbers (p179, p182)

**A powerful guarantee can triple sales, and it is called upon less than 5% of the time.**

## The deeper psychology (pp. 179–180)

In any transaction there is risk, and usually the **buyer** carries it. For your prospect the
biggest risk is **believing your promise.**

> **Go beyond thinking of the guarantee as risk reversal meant to push fence-sitters. Think of
> it as a PROOF ELEMENT — proof of how confident you are about delivering on every promise the
> offer makes.**

"For every dishonest scammer who takes advantage, you'll get **five honest prospects** who say
yes when they otherwise would have been too uneasy."

## The one-two punch (p177)

> **"You want to lead with the Godfather Strategy — an offer so good they can't refuse. And
> then back that up with the whole 'you can't lose' guarantee, and reverse all that risk."**

## The 7 steps (pp. 181–183)

1. **Study the competition.** Google your industry + "guarantee". Have someone **call and ask**.
   Look outside your industry and geography. Performance-related, or vanilla "satisfaction
   guaranteed"?
2. **Laser in on your strengths.** **"Always sell the thing you do better than your
   competition."**
3. **Be specific.** Not vague "satisfaction" — too broad. They must experience the benefits
   **or they don't owe you a red cent.** **"Guarantee in detail what that satisfaction will
   look like and make it measurable."**
4. **Choose a payback.** Fewer than 5% claim it, so make it attractive — **costs little,
   carries high perceived value**, and should **exceed expectations, be memorable, and wow
   them.**
5. **Test, measure, refine.** Some landing pages and not others. Test 30 days against 90
   (**longer guarantees generally work better**); even ten-year or lifetime as an experiment.
6. **Put it front and centre** — everywhere a customer will see it.
7. **Give it a name.** Dramatises it and heightens impact.

### Names (p184)
100% Money-Back · Triple-Protection · Take it to the Bank! · Better than Risk Free · You Can't
Lose · Unconditional · No Questions Asked · Iron-Clad · 30-Day Free Examination · My 110% 'Call
Me Crazy' GUARANTEE! · No Pussyfooting Around · Stronger than Steel

> **Register warning.** Most of that list is loud. `BRAND-VOICE.md` is quiet authority, no
> shouting. **Naming the guarantee is right; naming it "Call Me Crazy" is not.**

### The industry-definers (p185)

| Name | Promise |
|---|---|
| **The Forever Guarantee** — Cutco | Not satisfied at any time, we correct it or replace it |
| **The $1 Million Service Guarantee** — LifeLock | Up to $1M on lawyers, investigators, whatever it takes |
| **Domino's Pizza** | Delivered in 30 minutes… or it's FREE |
| **Costco** | 100% satisfaction; $55 membership refunded any time |

- Create a **FEATURE LIST**
- Create a **BENEFIT LIST**
- Reduce your **OFFER** to writing
- Offer a **POWER GUARANTEE**

**3 · Back up your big promise.** Explain the headline plainly in the **subheadline**.

**4 · Create irresistible intrigue.** Write **10 to 20** additional headlines, trim to the
**best six**. Each bullet calls out pains, fears, hopes and dreams and adds intrigue — *"The
Little-Known Secret to ____ that ____ Don't Want You to Know."*

**5 · Shine a floodlight on the problem.** *(Fabian: **bullet points = money sauce**.)* Explain
**vividly** how it feels to have the problem, until they say **"Yeah, that's exactly how I
feel."** Agitate it. **People are much more motivated by moving away from pain than towards
pleasure.** Then describe in vivid detail **what they've tried before and why it didn't work.**

**6 · Provide the solution.** *(Fabian: headline + subheadline · SEO · services.)* Prove it is
the best viable option and **how it differs from everything they've already tried and failed.**

**7 · Show your credentials.** Results, case studies, prestigious clients, number of customers,
press, awards. They must feel **"you've been there and done that."**

**8 · Detail the benefits.** **"Features tell and benefits sell, so talk only in benefits."**
Two-column list.

**9 · Social proof.** Third-party validation — research statistics or quotes from credible
sources.

**10 · Make your Godfather Offer.** Clear · value-based · concise · persuasive · **irresistible
— so compelling only a lunatic would refuse.**

**11 · Add bonuses.** Highly desirable but **not essential** to the outcome.

**12 · Stack the value.** Total it, then paint the picture that raises perceived value.

**13 · Reveal your price.** Add it up, reveal cheaper. **If pitching a free consult, put a
dollar value on what it is worth.**

**14 · Inject scarcity.** **Must be genuine.**

**15 · Give a powerful guarantee.** Money-back **or a promise not to share their information**
both qualify.

**16 · Call to action.** **A command.** Specific. **Ask them to do just one thing.**

**17 · Close with a P.S.** **The third most read element.** Remind of the offer, **warn of the
consequence of not acting**, include the CTA, remind of the limit.
